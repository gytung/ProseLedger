## Human evaluation: source recognition and revision preference

We conducted two short, blinded comparison exercises with course classmates. The organiser estimates that approximately ten classmates contributed, including one person who completed both exercises. Shared access codes were used, and the organiser recalls approximately two items per person. The exports preserve responses by item but not a recoverable assignment of every item to a distinct participant. We therefore report pooled judgement counts and treat the recruitment figure as an estimate; we do not use ten as the number of statistically independent raters.

The source-recognition exercise paired one published-paper excerpt with one passage labelled as generated by the project. Participants selected the published excerpt or “Cannot tell.” The ten pairs had five published excerpts in position A and five in position B. Generated passages averaged 66.5 words, versus 63.4 for published passages; their respective ranges were 42–96 and 40–99 words. Published material came from six identified ACL Anthology papers. This is a test of recognition against operational provenance labels: publication does not establish that a text was written without AI assistance.

The exported answers contained four correct selections, five incorrect selections and one “Cannot tell.” Correct selections accounted for 4/10 of all judgements, or 4/9 when the indecisive response was excluded. In five decisions the generated passage was selected as the published excerpt. Neither figure describes a percentage of people fooled, and the result does not demonstrate general indistinguishability. Optional ratings of specificity, evidence integration, restraint, coherence and naturalness were not completed; all 100 potential rating entries were empty and were excluded rather than imputed.

The separate fluency exercise asked which of an original passage and its retained revision sounded more natural. The finalised application reported four preferences for the revision, five for the original and one “Cannot tell”: 40% revision preference over all judgements and 44.4% over decisive judgements. “Cannot tell” is reported separately, not treated as evidence of equivalence. These observations do not establish an aggregate preference for the revised prose. The raw export retains A/B choices but omits the original/revised position key; the aggregate mapping was checked against the application's final result display. It cannot be reconstructed independently for every pair from this export alone.

The source exercise includes reused development material: four generated excerpts match passages in the archived development manuscript after punctuation and word normalisation. Some recur unchanged across several development outputs. The fluency export contains no passage text or model/run identifier. Consequently, these exercises provide complementary user observations, not a held-out test of the current public workflow or a direct human validation of the four-document GPTZero cohort.

Written comments raised concrete concerns about sentence length, repeated sentence structures and the explanation of reasoning. One comment described a pair as showing no difference. These comments inform future editing criteria, but punctuation and pronoun choices alone are not reliable source labels. A follow-up study should preserve anonymous participant identifiers, exact text-to-run mappings and randomised pair order, while separating blinded clarity judgements from verified meaning preservation.
